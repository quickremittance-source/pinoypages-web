# Self-employed in Canada: CPP contributions explained (2026 tax year)

_From Pinoy Pages 2026–2027_

When you work for an employer, CPP (Canada Pension Plan) comes off every paycheque and your employer pays the same amount again. **When you're self-employed, you pay both halves yourself**, usually when you file your tax return. For many Pinoys who go independent, this is the "surprise" on their first self-employed tax bill.

> 👉 **Try it:** our free [self-employed CPP estimator](https://pinoypages.ca/tools/cpp-estimator) _[URL TBC]_ uses the official 2026 rates below. It gives an estimate only.

## Who pays

With very few exceptions, everyone **over 18** who works in Canada **outside Quebec** and earns more than **$3,500** a year contributes to CPP. If you're self-employed, your contributions are based on your **net business income (after expenses)**. You **don't** pay CPP on other income, like investment earnings.

## The official 2026 numbers

| Item                                                                                       | 2026          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------- |
| Basic exemption (first $3,500 is not charged)                                              | **$3,500**    |
| Year's Maximum Pensionable Earnings (YMPE)                                                 | **$74,600**   |
| Year's Additional Maximum Pensionable Earnings (YAMPE)                                     | **$85,000**   |
| **Self-employed rate**, base + first additional (on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600)   | **11.90%**    |
| Self-employed maximum, base + first additional                                             | **$8,460.90** |
| **Self-employed rate, CPP2** (on earnings between $74,600 and $85,000)                     | **8.00%**     |
| Self-employed maximum, CPP2                                                                | **$832.00**   |
| _(For comparison: employee rate 5.95% and 4.00%; employee maximums $4,230.45 and $416.00)_ |               |

## Worked examples (simple arithmetic using the 2026 figures above)

These examples assume all the income is net self-employment income and there's no employment income. Your real numbers will differ, especially if you also had a job with CPP deducted.

| Net self-employment income (2026) | Calculation                          | Approx. CPP             |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------- |
| $30,000                           | ($30,000 − $3,500) × 11.9%           | **$3,153.50**           |
| $50,000                           | ($50,000 − $3,500) × 11.9%           | **$5,533.50**           |
| $80,000                           | $8,460.90 + ($80,000 − $74,600) × 8% | **$8,892.90**           |
| $85,000 or more                   | $8,460.90 + $832.00                  | **$9,292.90** (maximum) |

## Is any of it deductible?

Yes, partly. You calculate self-employed CPP on **Schedule 8**. Part of it is claimed as a **deduction** (line 22200), which lowers your taxable income, and part as a **non-refundable tax credit** (line 31000). Tax software or your preparer handles the split.

## If you also have a job

If you had employment income with CPP already deducted **and** self-employment income, Schedule 8 combines both, so you don't pay more than the yearly maximum. Bring all your T4 slips to your preparer.

## Planning tips

- **Budget for it.** Setting aside a share of each payment you receive can soften the April 30 bill. CPP is due with your income tax by **April 30, 2027**, even though self-employed people can file until June 15.
- **Instalments.** If the CRA asks you to pay instalments, your CPP is part of that calculation.
- **It's your pension.** CPP contributions build your future CPP retirement pension. For Filipinos with work history back home, Canada and the Philippines have a **social security agreement** that may help you qualify for some Canadian benefits. Check canada.ca for details.
