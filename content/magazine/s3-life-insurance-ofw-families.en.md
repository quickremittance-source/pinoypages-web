# Two countries, two policies, one family: a life insurance review

Rosa Mabini has a name for the folder that new clients bring to her office in Mississauga. She calls it the archive.

"It's usually a plastic envelope," she says. "There's a policy from a company in Manila, bought while someone was working in Hong Kong or Dubai. There's a group plan from a job here. Sometimes there's a third thing a relative sold them at a party. Nobody has read any of it in years."

Families who spent time working overseas before settling in Canada often accumulate coverage this way, piecemeal and for good reasons at the time. The difficulty is that nobody steps back to see how the pieces fit together. Mabini, who runs [Padayon Financial Planning](/listing/ins-06) and spent six years working in Hong Kong herself, begins every engagement by assembling that picture before recommending anything.

The first question is what each policy actually is. Term insurance covers a fixed period and is generally the least expensive way to protect a family while children are young or a mortgage is outstanding. Permanent policies, such as whole life, last indefinitely and accumulate a cash value, at a considerably higher premium. Neither is inherently superior; they solve different problems.

The second question concerns group coverage through an employer. It is convenient, and frequently it is the largest policy a family holds, but it usually ends when the employment does. Workers who change jobs often, or who rely on agency contracts, may discover the gap only when it matters.

The third, and most frequently neglected, is the beneficiary designation. Marriages, separations and the birth of children rarely prompt anyone to update the paperwork. Mabini has seen policies still naming a parent who has since passed away.

Policies issued in the Philippines raise their own practical questions. A claim may require original documents, notarised translations or a representative in the country, and premiums paid from Canada can lapse quietly if a bank account changes. None of this argues for cancelling such a policy. It argues for knowing how it would be claimed.

"I've had clients who were about to buy a new policy here that duplicated one they already had," says Liza Reyes of [Hiraya Insurance & Mortgage](/listing/ins-01) in Etobicoke. "The cheapest insurance is the coverage you already own and understand."
